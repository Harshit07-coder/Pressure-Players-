Problem: Identify the Most Reliable Players
Under Pressure

Build a data-driven system that measures how batters and bowlers perform in high-pressure match situations compared with their usual performance levels. Use your system to identify and rank the players who deliver most consistently when it matters.

My Methodology

I analysed the whole dataset which was provided to me (It contains T20 & T20I Data since 2022 (Excluding T20I WC Data)), To identify best players under pressure it means we need to find out who plays the best when the match is tight and what defines the match is tight and not the normal easy condition, because Anyone can score runs when the match is easy. A batter who hits 50 when the team is almost to victory is not in the same situation as a batter who hits 50 when the team needs 11 an over and just 3 overs left.

So according to me the whole project is about one thing: how do we put a number on pressure? Once we can say “this ball had a pressure of 0.9 and that ball had a pressure of 0.2”, we can look only at the high-pressure balls and see who did well on them.

To Put Pressure Number on Balls and to filter high pressure balls, I followed these 3 Steps:

1. Given every ball a pressure number between 0 to 1, using the combination of runs left, wickets left, balls left and the score.

2. Then Multiplied by a match weight: an IPL final has more pressure than an IPL league game similarly a game against England will have more pressure than a game against a weak team like Zimbabwe.

3. Kept only balls which had the final pressure score of greater than or equal to 0.7, then judge each player only on those balls played.

Explanation of Key Methodologies used to identify Pressure Balls:

1. Value Added (Current Runs Vs Expected Runs Per Ball) Using ICC DLS Table

DLS stands for Duckworth-Lewis-Stern, It was invented for rain affected games, to reach to result of the match, it tells if a team has this many overs left and this many wickets left, then according to DLS the current score is below or par the Expected score.

Think of a team’s innings as a fuel tank. At the start the tank is 100% full. Every ball you play uses a bit of fuel (overs). Every wicket you lose also destroys fuel.

DLS Table is already Provided by ICC; it tells according to current overs left and wickets left how much resources left for team to use in the form of percentage.

To turn percentages into expected runs at the end of inns we need to know how many runs 100% resources left gives. We call that par value, the average first-innings score, I Kept average score calculations according to the given dataset that is from 2022 only, and it was about 171 for T20Is and about 183 for T20 league games, adjusted a little for each venue because some grounds are high scoring and some are low.

Example in a T20I (par 171): 10 overs left and 5 wickets in hand has 46.1% resources left, which is 0.461 x 171 = about 79 runs. That is what a team scores in rest of the 10 overs from here on according to that particular venue and T20I matches data since 2022, So DLS vs expected just means comparing how much the team scored Vs How much the team was expected to score according to venue and T20I history, so If a team is expected to score 79 more runs from here, and they score 100, they beat the DLS expectation. If they score 60, they fell short.

Value Added is did this ball push the expected end score up or down, it is calculated using 4 steps:

1. The Ground’s AVG Score (Par Score): For every ground we take all the first innings played there in the data and average the scores. That is the venue average. A ground with only a few matches is blended toward the format average so we do not trust two games too much.

2. Resources left from the DLS table: It returns the percentage of the innings still to play ball by ball, take an Example: 10 overs left and 6 wickets in hand = 50.0%. The ICC DLS table only has whole overs (0,1,2,3,4,5,….20). For a ball in between (59 balls left which is 9 overs and 5 balls) we draw a straight line between the two nearest rows: 10 overs left = 50.0%, 9 overs left = 46.1%, so 59 balls left = 50.0 - (50.0 - 46.1) / 6 = 49.35%, It tells Resources left% per ball.

3. Expected end score at the end of the Inns: score now + (resources left / 100) x venue average

4. Value added = expected end score AFTER the ball minus BEFORE the ball

Suppose expected end score at the end of inns is 165, before the ball is being bowled, then if the batsman hit the four, so expected end score at the end of inns became 168.4, so value added is (168.4-165 which is +2.89), suppose if he gets bowled in that ball that is wicket + dot ball which will make value added in negative like -7.1 approx., this is how Value added works for each ball.

Changes in Value Added IN 2ND Inns: In Expected end score at the end of the Inns formula which is: score now + (resources left / 100) x venue average, in 2nd inns instead of venue average we use Target as Teams play according to the target they are defending or chasing not what how ground behaves.

Changes in Value Added for Bowlers: Everything same it’s just what is positive for batsmen will be negative for bowlers and vice versa, A dot ball would be +1.11 for bowler. Conceding a four will give them negative value like -2.89. A credited wicket on a dot ball can boost like +7.6.

2. Pressure On Every Ball

Pressure depends on whether it is the first or the second innings

For Run Chases (2nd Inns)

In a chase we know the target, so we can measure how hard the job is. We call the difficulty number D: D = runs still needed / runs normally expected from that situation DLS Expected Inns end score for 2nd inns (score now + (resources left / 100) x Target), Example: 10 overs left, 5 wickets in hand, expected end score at 2nd inns end is 171. Suppose Runs needed from there is 79 according to DLS Expected end score. And now also If the team needs 79 more to win, D = 1.0 (normal chase). If they need 120, D = 120/79 = 1.5 (hard). If they need only 40, D = 0.5 (easy), Also When a chase becomes hopeless (D above 2) we slowly lower it, because nobody can feel pressure about a game that is already gone. For the bowler it is the opposite: when few runs are needed and many wickets are in hand (more D), more runs and less wickets left will be (less D) for Bowlers, For bowlers also When D goes to Below 0.3 it lowers the D value because of match already gone no pressure.

Example: Chasing team: 62 runs needed, 4 overs left, 4 wickets down (6 in hand). Par (DLS 2ND INNS Formula) for T20 is 183.Resources left = 23.3%, so normal runs from here = 42.6. D = 62 / 42.6 = 1.45. Batter pressure = 0.92 (high: 62 is a lot more than normal). Bowler pressure = 0.18 (low: the batting side is struggling, so the bowler is relaxed). If the batter hits a six on this ball: the ball’s break-even is 1.62 runs, so value added = 6 - 1.62 = +4.4. If he gets out instead, the wicket here only takes about 1.5 runs off the expected end score.

For 1st Innings

There is no target in the first innings, so we ask: is the team ahead of or behind where it should be? We compute the DLS 1st Inns projected score (score now + (resources left / 100) x venue average). If that projection is far under venue average 1st inns score in recent matches according to data, the batters are in trouble. If it is far above venue average 1st inns score in recent matches according to data, the bowlers are in Trouble.

3 Rules To get More accurate Pressure weight on each ball:

i. Pressure is multiplied by 0.8 for the whole first innings: pressure is always more in the second innings. A big first-innings score is easier than winning a hard chase for example Babar Azam 100 of 68 balls in 1st Inns is far easy then Virat Kohli’s 82 Runs off 53 balls when Required Run Rate was mounting and less wickets left, so 1st inns performance I penalized to uplift 2nd inns performance.

ii. Powerplay balls multiplied by 0.8, middle overs by 0.9, death overs by 1.0: As pressure increases From Powerplay to slowly slowly when the innings is going to end, so death overs performance should be uplifted than compared to powerplay.

iii. Knockout innings (IPL knockout, Asia Cup final) get a minimum start point of 0.5: So, a great knock in a final count even if the team was never in trouble

3. How much does the match matter?

Whatever the Pressure score is in the particular Ball gets Multiplied by Match Weight, so a performance in A Knockout gets more value than a league game, in the provided dataset T20I WC was not present, my Match weight was this:

The ICC top 10 rule: In national-team matches (T20I, tri-series, Asia Cup) only games against a top 10 team get the full weight. Current top 10 is: 1 England, 2 India, 3 Australia, 4 New Zealand, 5 South Africa, 6 Pakistan, 7 West Indies, 8 Bangladesh, 9 Sri Lanka, 10 Afghanistan. A match against The Pressure Players method, explained simply Page 9 any other team is weighted 0.85, below an IPL league match. Against a top 10 team the weight is also adjusted by rank: x1.08 against England, going down by 0.012 for each rank, to x0.97 against Afghanistan

Also, A batter’s opponent is the bowling team and A bowler’s opponent is the batting team, So the same match can count fully for one player and less for another, For Eg Ireland Bowler will get more points for performing against India and even if Indian batsman gets a century, it won’t count much.

This is how pressure score gets counted on each ball:

Final ball score = pressure score x match weight. A ball is “under pressure” if this is 0.7 or more., only performance under those balls gets counted whose pressure score is greater than or equal to 0.7

Example: suppose the ball has pressure 0.92. In an IPL league game (weight 1.0) the final score is 0.92, so it counts. In an IPL knockout (weight 1.8) the score is 1.66. In a T20I against a team outside the top 10 (weight 0.85) it is 0.78, which still counts but with less weight. In an IPL knockout, a ball needs a pressure of only about 0.39 to count (1.8 x 0.39 = 0.7), which makes sense because everything is a big moment in a knockout match

Every batter metric

For all batter metrics, we only use pressure balls (final score 0.7 or more) and every ball is weighted by its score. A batter must have faced at least 150 pressure balls to be ranked. Higher is better

Bar length is the weight each metric gets in the batter’s rating (the weights add up to 100%).

1. Value added

It is the best single number. It rewards runs, punishes dots and getting out, and knows the expected runs on that ball compare it with actual runs scored in that ball, all at once. That is why it has the biggest weight,

PS: Detailed explanation I have given above.

2. Pressure Runs

How many runs batsman scored in the hard moments, where harder moments count more, it is calculated using Sum of (runs on a ball x that ball’s score) over all pressure balls, 30 runs on balls that each had 1.0 pressure score = 30 pressure runs. The same 30 runs on balls that each scored 0.75 pressure score = 22.5 pressure runs, this metric rewards volume, someone who does it again and again beats someone who did it once.

3. Strike Rate

How fast he scores in the hard moments, example: 30 runs off 20 balls = strike rate 150, it rewards batsman who maintain good strike rate under pressure balls or you can say pressure situations.

4. Boundary%

How often batsman hits boundary in pressure balls, example: 8 fours and 4 sixes in 80 balls = 12/80 = 15%.

5. Dot Ball %

Lower it is better it is, it is calculated using 100 x (balls with 0 runs) / pressure balls faced, it uplift batsman who play less dot balls under pressure.

6. Win Share

How many of batsman pressure runs came in matches his team won, (pressure runs scored in matches his team won) / (all his pressure runs), for example: Batsman scored 100 pressure runs: 60 in wins and 40 in losses, so his win share is 60%, Runs that help win the match matter more than runs in a lost cause. It carries only 9% because one batter does not decide a match alone.

7. Dismissal Rate

It tells How often batsman gets out under pressure, it is calculated using Number of dismissals per 100 pressure balls, adjusted for small samples, lower it is better it is.

8. Chase Win Credit

Bonus points for helping your team win a hard chase, for example Team chases 180 and wins. then we apply this formula: chase win credit = 40 x (his share of the team’s chase runs) x (how hard the chase was) x (match weight). Suppose Hardness = 0.7. He scores 45 of the 180 = 25% share. IPL league weight 1.0. Credit = 40 x 0.25 x 0.7 x 1.0 = 7.0., This metric will give more value to Innings like Virat Kohli’s 72 off 53 balls against Pakistan in 2022 T20I WC at Melbourne.

Every bowler metric

For bowlers we use pressure deliveries (final score 0.7 or more, from the bowler’s side). A bowler needs at least 150 legal balls under pressure to get rated. Bowling pressure is high when the batting team is in control (low D value in a chase) or scoring faster than avg 1st inns score at that venue in the first innings.

Bar length is the weight each metric gets in the bowler’s rating (adds up to 100%)

1. Value added (higher is better)

Bowler score card for how much he lowered the batting team’s expected end score on each ball, It rewards economy, dots and wickets together, and a wicket is worth more when the batters had a lot of innings left, Detailed explanation I have provided above

2. Defence Credit (higher is better)

It is Bonus points for helping your team defend a total when the chase got close, It is the bowler version of the chase-win credit: a bowler who helps defend a total that was nearly chased down deserves more than one whose team won by miles, it is calculated using For every match his team won while defending: credit = 40 x (his share of the team’s bowling value added) x (how close the chase got) x (match weight).

3. Wickets per ball (higher is better)

How often the bowler takes a wicket under pressure, example: 2 wickets in 30 balls = 6.7 per 100 balls.

4. Economy (lower is better)

How much runs bowler concede under pressure, for example 12 runs in 12 balls = 1 run per ball = economy 6 (1*6)

5. Dot Ball % (higher is better)

Higher it is better it is, it is calculated using 100 x (balls with 0 runs) / pressure balls bowled, it uplift bowlers who ball more dot balls under pressure.

6. Pressure Wickets (higher is better)

The total quality of his wickets, not just the count, Sum of the pressure ball scores on the balls where he took a credited wicket, for example One wicket on a ball scoring 1.6 (IPL knockout, tense) counts 1.6. A wicket on a ball scoring 0.75 counts 0.75, it Rewards volume of big-moment wickets, it is just like Pressure Runs Metric for Batsmen

7. Win Share (higher is better)

How many of bowler’s pressure deliveries came in matches his team won. Example 60 of his 100 pressure deliveries were in wins: 60% win share

8. Yorker % (higher is better)

How often the bowler balls Yorker Under Pressure, example 3 yorkers in 24 pressure balls = 12.5% Yorker %
